## Redundancy inside the node.

Accelerators are specified in pairs. A card failure costs headroom, not service — the workload continues on the survivor while the part is replaced.

## Nodes as peers, not islands.

Because each node runs far below capacity, nodes serve several sites and can carry each other’s. The fleet is N+1 by arithmetic rather than by buying a spare for every location.

## Maintenance in parallel.

Updates, model changes and hardware work are staged on one node while another serves. There is no maintenance window in which the business stops.

## Ride-through on the electrical side.

The UPS covers a transient and provides orderly shutdown on a sustained outage. Generator capacity is an option, not a requirement, because the load is about a kilowatt rather than a data hall.

## Independent of the circuit.

A cut fibre or an ISP incident takes a cloud-dependent operation off the air entirely; here it takes away remote access while the business, the sensing and the control loops keep running on site.

## Failures seen before they become outages.

The operational layer watches the conditions that cause on-premises failures — thermal rise, power anomalies, water, airflow loss — and raises them while there is still time to act.

---

Set against a cloud service, the comparison is not availability percentage against availability percentage. A cloud agreement offers a credit when the service is lost; it does not offer continuity, and the failure domain is somebody else’s and invisible. Here the failure domains are small, local, observable and owned.
